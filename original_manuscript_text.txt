ABSTRACT 
The increasing concentration of atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO₂) has intensified 
global efforts to develop Carbon Capture, Storage and Utilisation (CCSU) technologies. 
This review examines key CO₂ utilisation routes, including chemical transformation, 
mineralisation and biological transformation. Major CO₂ products such as methanol, 
urea, formic acid, dimethyl carbonate, and polycarbonates, are discussed with respect to 
reaction mechanisms, catalysts, industrial processes, and applications. Emerging uses in 
pharmaceutical and biotechnological industries, where CO₂ is used as a green reagent 
and solvent, are also discussed. Recent trends indicate emerging interest in industry and 
the adoption of technology, but issues such as high energy demand, catalyst constraints, 
and economic limitations remain a point of concern. To improve efficiency and 
scalability, there is a need to integrate renewable energy sources with sophisticated 
catalytic systems. Overall, CO₂ utilisation presents a promising pathway toward a 
circular carbon economy and supports global efforts to achieve net-zero emissions and 
increase possibilities of continued use of fossil fuels such as coal, petroleum and natural 
gas. This paper highlights recent advances (2024–2026) and identifies key challenges

for large-scale implementation.

1.​ Introduction 
Carbon dioxide (CO₂), long considered a major greenhouse gas contributing to 
climate change, is increasingly being recognized as an important carbon feedstock 
in the chemical, energy, and materials industries (Aresta et al., 2014; Centi & 
Perathoner, 2009; Sharma, 2021; Kim et al., 2022). Emissions of CO₂ worldwide 
exceed 35 billion tonnes per year (IEA, 2024), most of which are from power 
generation, steel, cement, chemicals, and transport sectors (IEA, 2024). As long as 
fossil fuels remain the main source of energy, there is a dire need to develop policies 
to reduce CO₂ levels whilst simultaneously expanding industrial production (Olah et 
al., 2011; Aresta et al., 2014; Sharma,2023). Carbon Capture, Storage and 
Utilisation (CCSU) methods offer a two-fold advantage by reducing emissions and 
enabling the continued use of fossil fuels by converting CO₂ into value-added 
products. This review repositions CO₂ from an environmental liability to a strategic 
carbon resource (Keith et al., 2018). The high thermodynamic stability of CO₂ 
(ΔG°f = -394.4 kJ/mol) makes its activation kinetically challenging (Lackner, 2003; 
Sharma, 2010), yet catalysis, electrochemistry, and biotechnology techniques help 
overcome these barriers. One example is chemical changes, such as dry reforming of 
methane and CO₂ to produce syngas (H₂ + CO), which can form hydrocarbons via 
Fischer-Tropsch synthesis (Pakhare & Spivey, 2014). Bioconversion uses 
microorganisms such as Moorella thermoacetica to convert organic acids, which can 
then be mineralized into construction materials using fly ash or steel slag in a 
circular economy (Bagotia et al., 2018). Several techniques for the chemical and 
 


=== PAGE 3 ===
 
bioconversion of CO2 have been suggested recently along with the directions for 
future research work for developing circular economy using CO2 (Sharma, 2021; 
Sharma ,2023). The current trends (2024-2026) indicate the pace as the currently 
existing global market of CO₂ utilisation is USD 4.57 billion in 2024 and will 
become USD 11.15 billion in 2032 at a CAGR of 13.6% (IEA, 2024), whereas the 
larger markets of utilizing carbon capture undergo expansions of USD 3.5 billion in 
2026 and USD 51.6 billion in 2050 (IPCC,2023), Some of the driving aspects are 
growing global climate policies, where more than 474 CCSU projects have been 
announced, aiming to reach the eight hundred and fifteen million tonnes per annum 
by 2030 (Global CCS Institute, 2024), and investment such as the United States 
Inflation Reduction Act allocating one point seven billion to capture demonstrations. 
The Icelandic Mammoth DAC i.e., Direct Air Capture (launched in 2026, capturing 
36,000 tonnes/year) and the DAC unit at Aramco (12 tonnes/year in 2025) are 
among the first steps. This need is further supported by the fact that 53,970 
publications on CCSU in 2024 are 11.4% higher than the previous year (Global CCS 
Institute, 2024). This review compiles findings from chemical pathways, industrial 
case studies, and new trends to highlight the role of CO₂ in advancing the 
low-carbon economy. Since there are extensive reserves of coal, a cheaper source of 
energy available in the world, therefore, there is a grave need for exploring 
economical possibilities of their continued use for power generation. That would 
become possible only if the CCSU technologies are economically established.

This study is a review-based analysis of carbon dioxide (CO₂) utilisation

3.​ Results and Discussion 
(1)​Objective and Scope 
This review aims to analyse key pathways for CO₂ utilisation, including 
hydrogenation, carboxylation, and reforming processes. It assesses the industrial 
processes, catalysts, and reaction mechanisms of transforming CO₂ to 
value-added products. The research also contrasts the leading CO₂-based 
chemicals, including methanol, urea, formic acid, dimethyl carbonate, metal 
carbonates and polycarbonates, in terms of their sustainability and industrial 
applicability. Recent developments and future trends are also encompassed in 
the scope, as well as emerging applications in pharmaceuticals, biotechnology 
and construction materials. 
(2)​CO₂ as a Resource  
Various industries emit large amounts of CO₂, and it is inexpensive and 
non-toxic, making it an appealing chemical feedstock. Nonetheless, it needs to 
be activated by catalysts, heat, or renewable electricity to achieve 
thermodynamic stability (Alper & Orhan, 2017). There could also be a need for 
the costly hydrogen under pressure to hydrogenate CO2 uneconomically. Efforts 
are required to avoid using hydrogen to the extent possible though it is not 
possible always and thus this may be done to the extent possible economically.

CO₂ utilisation contributes to sustainability by reducing greenhouse gas 
emissions, replacing fossil-derived chemicals with CO₂-based alternatives, and 
enabling circular carbon economy models such as CO₂-based gasification 
processes for syngas production. It helps in enabling circular carbon models, 
e.g., CO₂ and coal gasification (Dhawan et al., Pakhare & Spivey, 2014; Sharma 
and Giri, 2016; Dhawan et al., 2020), to produce syngas (Centi & Perathoner, 
2009). 
According to historical reviews, CO₂ was considered an inert gas (Peters et al., 
2011; Quadrelli et al., 2011); however, it is now widely used in urea production 
(on an industrial scale) and in biofuels with potential. New trends are moving 
more rapidly: the EU launched an Innovation Fund worth 1.5 billion to support 
CCSU projects, and China, in its Five-Year Plan, is interested in CCSU, 
increasing DAC and utilisation (Global CCS Institute, 2024). Indian 
Government has committed Rs. 20,000 crores towards efforts on CCSU for the 
2026 -2027 budget 
(https://www.downtoearth.org.in/climate-change/indias-rs-20000-crore-CCSU-p

According to the Global Carbon Budget, global CO₂ emissions were projected to 
reach 41.6 billion tonnes by 2024, underscoring the need for CCU (IEA,2024) 
and these have understandably increased further in 2026.  
Carbon dioxide is thermodynamically stable due to its linear molecular structure 
and strong C=O double bonds (~750 kJ/mol bond energy), with a standard Gibbs 
free energy of formation of ΔGf∘(CO₂) = −394.4 kJ/mol. This high stability 
implies a large activation energy barrier, kinetic inertness under ambient

sources include hydrogen production (especially if not green), high pressure

4.​ Chemical Conversion of CO₂  
The activation of stable CO₂ is carried out through several chemical means. Green 
hydrogenation using H₂ occurs at 200–300°C and 20–100 bar using Cu/ZnO 
catalysts (Goeppert et al., 2014; Bowker et al., 2019; Sabatier, 1912, Kim et al., 
2022). Another method is formic acid reduction by renewable electricity using Sn 
electrodes, while sunlight-based photocatalysis often utilizes TiO₂-based materials 
(Whipple & Kenis, 2010). In the field of polymers, activation involves 
carboxylation/cycloaddition (Kätelhön et al., 2019), and metal-organic frameworks 
(MOFs) are employed to enhance selectivity in the cycloaddition reaction of 
epoxides (Elhenawy et al., 2020).

Dry reforming of methane using CO2 (CH4 + CO₂ → 2CO + 2H₂) involves Ni-based 
catalysts, and Fe promoters minimize the coke formation (Pakhare & Spivey, 2014; 
Manabayeva et al. ,2023). Several bimetallic and trimetallic catalysts along with Ni

Methane bi-reforming (BRM) has shown promise, with conversion rates exceeding

5.​ Industrially Relevant CO₂-Derived Chemicals  
(1)​Methanol 
Reaction: CO₂ + 3H₂ → CH₃OH + H₂O (ΔH = -49 kJ/mol).  
Catalysts: Cu/ZnO/Al₂O₃ (standard industrial process).  
Procedure: feed (CO₂) (flue gas/DAC) and green H₂ (generated in the electrolysis); 
reaction conditions : 200–300°C, 20-100 bar; pathway; CO₂ → formate → 
formaldehyde → methoxy → methanol; distillation; recycle unreacted gases. 
(Goeppert et al., 2014; Behrens et al., 2012, Kim et al., 2022). 
It is used as a fuel additive and as a precursor to formaldehyde, acetic acid, and 
olefins (in the MTO process). Ethanol has potential as a fuel derived from CO₂, and 
pilots such as Oxylus' electrochemical conversion are scheduled for 2026.

(1)​ Carboxylation Reactions 
One of the most basic reactions of CO₂ in the synthesis of organic compounds is 
carboxylation. 
General Reaction: R-MgX + CO₂ - R-COOH. 
In this reaction, organometallic reagents such as Grignard reagents (R-MgX) or 
organolithium compounds react with CO₂. The resulting magnesium carboxylate 
is acidified to get carboxylic acids. 
Medical Significance: These carboxylic acids are key intermediates in the 
synthesis of NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen-type molecules), antibiotics, antifungals, 
and β-lactam drugs. This reaction enables the efficient synthesis of CO₂, which 
is a cost-effective source of carbon.   
(2)​Carbamate and Urea Formation 
CO₂ readily reacts with amines to form carbamates and urea. 
General Reaction: 2 RNH₂ + CO₂ - RNH-CO-NHR + H₂O. 
Pharmaceutical Significance: Antimalarial drugs, antibiotics, antiretroviral 
drugs, antidiabetic drugs, and enzymes are produced. Examples include prodrug 
formation (carbamates shield amino groups) and drug stability (in fact, the urea 
derivatives enhance bioavailability).  
(3)​Reactions in Supercritical CO₂ (scCO₂)  
Supercritical CO₂ (scCO₂) above 31.1°C and 73.8 bar is a dual solvent, both 
liquid and gas, and is considered a green solvent. 
Some of the applications in pharmaceutical synthesis include: scCO₂ -assisted 
organic reactions, such as hydrogenation, oxidation, polymerization; particle 
formation, crystallization, or polymorph control to achieve solubility/stability; 
and many others.

Its Advantages include: Removing harmful organic solvents, processing at low 
temperatures to maintain heat-sensitive molecules, and high diffusivity, which 
permits faster, cleaner reactions. 
8.Pharmaceutical Uses of CO₂  
(1)​ Extraction and Purification 
CO₂ (at supercritical conditions) has numerous applications in the extraction of 
caffeine (decaffeination of coffee/tea) and essential oils, and is also used for the 
extraction of alkaloids, vitamins, and steroids. 
scCO₂ is a better choice because it produces no solvent residues (enhancing drug 
purity), is non-toxic and GRAS, and can be used to selectively extract 
compounds by adjusting pressure and temperature.  
(2)​Sterilization 
Supercritical CO₂ is an efficient sterilization medium for implantable medical 
devices, surgical equipment, biological materials, and temperature-sensitive drug 
formulations. Advantages over Heat or Radiation: Non-destructive sterilization; 
does not degrade polymeric materials or biomolecules; leaves residues of 
microbes or biomolecules. 
(3)​Formulation and Drug Delivery 
CO₂ is used to make porous structures, microparticles, foams for controlled drug 
release, nano- and microencapsulation, tissue engineering materials, and 
aerogels. 
Mechanisms: The fast growth of the supercritical solutions; foaming under the 
influence of CO₂; displacement of the solutions. 
 


=== PAGE 16 ===
 
Its Applications include the production of micronized drug powders with 
improved dissolution rates and CO₂-expanded solutions for injectable or 
transdermal formulations. 
(4)​ pH Control in Fermentation Processes 
In the production of biopharmaceuticals, CO₂ helps maintain optimal pH in 
fermenters, which is then bubbled into microbial or cell culture systems. The 
control of pH is essential for the production of antibiotics, enzymes, monoclonal 
antibodies, and therapeutic proteins. 
(5)​CO₂ as an Environmentally Friendly Solvent  
CO₂ is preferred as it is Non-flammable, chemically inert, and recyclable. 
Applications: Chromatography; particle engineering; drying processes; and 
pharmaceutical green chemistry. CO₂ enables safer, more sustainable production 
processes.

11.​ Sustainability, Challenges, & Future Prospects  
Although the use of CO₂ has enormous potential to transform the concept of a 
circular carbon economy, there is significant complexity surrounding its adoption 
across the technical, economic, regulatory, and environmental fronts. At the same 
time, new vistas arise from technological advancements, policy innovations, and 
market dynamics, offering a way out of these hurdles. It is a section that summarizes 
the critical issues and opportunities based on recent analyses (2024-2026) and 
focuses on sustainable scaling approaches. 
 


=== PAGE 18 ===
 
(1)​Technical Challenges 
The thermodynamic stability of CO₂ requires significant energy to be activated, 
leading to high operating costs and energy penalties in processes such as 
hydrogenation and electrochemical reduction. The capture rate of existing 
technologies typically reaches 90 %, so to reach 98 % or more, which would 
qualify as net-zero, larger equipment would be used, new steps in the process 
would be needed, and the energy consumption per tonne of CO₂ captured would 
increase, which in turn becomes a factor in inflating the costs of the units. 
Poisoning and catalyst degradation, especially in dry reforming, are ongoing 
problems, and coke formation reduces efficiency (Manabayeva et al. ,2023). In 
the case of direct air capture (DAC) and utilisation, the technology is not yet 
sufficiently advanced, and it is difficult to scale low-concentration CO₂ 
extraction and combine it with renewable energy sources. Recent research 
emphasizes that the innovations in materials, such as advanced membranes and 
sorbents (Global CCS Institute, 2024), will be required to reduce energy 
requirements by 20-30% (D’Alessandro et al., 2010). 
(2)​ Economic and Market Problems. 
The substantial capital and operating costs are very restrictive, and the cost of 
capturing CO₂ ranges from $40 to $ 120 per tonne, which is above current 
carbon prices and, in an unsubsidized business scenario, makes it difficult to 
cover the expense (IPCC ,2023). Next-wave technologies pass through "valleys 
of death," a lengthy development cycle, high capital expenditure, and untested 
business models that would discourage more traditional venture capital (World 
Economic Forum, 2025). The issue of fragmented markets and a lack of regular 
demand for CO₂-driven products also does not help the process of 
 


=== PAGE 19 ===
 
commercialization, as in the chemicals industry, where the utilisation route, such 
as CO₂-to-methanol, would require a 14% cost reduction to compete by 2030 
(Wood Mackenzie, 2026). There are also bottlenecks in monitoring, reporting, 
and verification (MRV) of utilisation chains that impede certification and entry 
to the market. 
(3)​Policy and Regulatory Problems 
Diverse systems across the global arena create ambiguity regarding liability, 
long-term storage tracking, and the transportation of CO₂ across borders, 
reducing investment confidence. Most of the regions do not have a unified 
approach to CCSU, and fragmented incentives result in uneven implementation - 
North America and Europe are the first, and Asia considers policy gaps. The 
community needs to be strongly involved because the population remains 
unconvinced due to environmental impacts and equity issues (IPCC ,2023). 
(4)​Environmental and Sustainability Challenges. 
Although CCU decreases emissions, life-cycle analyses of this method indicate 
potential trade-offs, including increased water use in mineralization or 
energy-intensive processes that offset the reductions if powered by fossil fuels 
(IEA, 2024). Scaling should consider geographic expansion, with expansion 
equally distributed to prevent stress on vulnerable populations (IPCC ,2023).

(5) Opportunities and Future Prospects. 
Nonetheless, 2026 will be a milestone in the life of CCU when its market will 
reach USD 6.45 million and reach USD 20.4 billion at a CAGR of 13.5 by 2035 
(IEA,2024). Cost and efficiency improvements will be enabled by technological 
innovations, such as reactive carbon capture (RCC) and chemical looping 
hydrogen production. Policy momentum: e.g., the EU Net-Zero Industry Act, 
which requires storage of 50 Mt over the five-year timeframe, and the IRA, 
which could unlock $80 billion in investment, could open up investment of $80 
billion. Durable revenue will be generated through integration with compliance 
carbon markets, which represent 28% of global emissions. Carbon-to-value 
products such as nanotubes (a high-potential segment with 10-fold growth by 
2030) and sustainable aviation fuels (another high-potential segment) are 
emerging uses of platinum compounds. By 2030, capture capacity may increase 
to 435 Mt/year, 4 times current levels, and DAC hubs such as Mammoth may 
reach gigaton-scale potential. The R&D of energy-efficient technology, 
harmonization of policies, and public-private collaboration should be the focus 
of the future so that the result can be a market of 70-550 billion dollars by 
2030-2040.

India’s Investment in Carbon Capture 
Recent policy developments highlight the growing global commitment toward 
carbon capture and utilisation technologies. In 2026, the Government of India 
announced a significant financial allocation of approximately USD 2.4 billion to 
accelerate the deployment of carbon capture, utilisation, and storage (CCSU) 
technologies across key industrial sectors. This initiative is aimed at facilitating 
large-scale implementation beyond pilot and demonstration projects, particularly 
in emissions-intensive industries such as steel, cement, and power generation. 
The funding strategy focuses on supporting innovation, infrastructure 
development, and commercialization of CCSU technologies, reflecting a shift 
from research-driven approaches to industrial-scale adoption. By prioritizing 
public financing and policy support, India aims to reduce emissions while 
maintaining industrial growth, aligning with its long-term net-zero 
commitments. Such initiatives indicate a broader global trend where 
governments are increasingly integrating CCSU into climate policy frameworks.  
12.​ Conclusion  
The use of CO₂ represents a paradigm shift, treating emissions as liabilities to the 
environment and using them as resources in a closed carbon economy. Synthesizing 
a variety of paths - chemical transformations to form methanol and urea, 
biotechnological transformations to produce biofuels, and mineralization to form 
long-lasting construction materials, this review has shown the versatility of CO₂ as 
the solution to climate problems and economic development. Its potential is further 
supported by advanced applications in the pharmaceutical sector, value-added 
chemical and agricultural studies, and by bibliometric trends of rapidly rising 
international research in hydrogenation and catalysis.

However, scaling is limited by high costs, energy requirements, and regulatory 
obstacles, as noted in recent studies The 2024-2026 momentum, though, growth to 
USD 11-22 billion by 2032-2033, investment of more than half a billion, and other 
projects such as Mammoth DAC are indicative of viability. By 2030, as capture 
capacity reaches 435 Mt/year and 2030 policies, such as the NZIA, are forced by the 
EU to push deployment, the CCU will mitigate 130 Mt/year in emissions (IEA 
,2023) and create the desired markets of 70-550 billion by 2030-2040. 
To achieve net-zero goals, stakeholders will have to focus on innovation in 
low-energy technologies, harmonized policies, and fair application. By using 
renewables and carbon credits, CO₂ emissions can guide fossil-based economies 
toward sustainable futures, ensuring environmental conservation and resilience in 
the industrial sector. In fact, coal is the major fuel of choice for much needed power 
generation. Coal is the cheapest fossil fuel which is abundantly available as a low 
cost fuel, may have to be utilized . If the cost-effective CCSU technologies become 
available then it may become environmentally cleaner to use coal and future 
generations may not have to depend only on renewable energy sources which have 
their own issues.
